# The Shape of a Page

Nobody reads a wall of text on purpose. The page — the physical, bounded
page — was one of the first user interfaces, and five centuries of printers
quietly optimized it before anyone thought to call it design.

A traditional book page holds between sixty and seventy characters per
line. Wider than that and the eye loses the return path to the next line;
narrower and the rhythm of reading turns choppy, all carriage return and no
sentence. The margins are not wasted space. They are where your thumbs go.

Printers speak of the *type area* sitting inside the page the way a
painting sits inside a mat. The inner margin is smallest but doubled by its
neighbor across the gutter; the outer margin gives the thumbs their
purchase; the bottom margin is largest, because a block of text set dead
center looks, to every human eye ever tested, like it is sliding off the
page.

None of this is nostalgia. It is accumulated measurement. When we format a
blog post into a book, we are not decorating it — we are borrowing five
hundred years of usability testing.

## What survives the trip

Not everything on a web page deserves to reach paper. Navigation, comment
threads, share buttons, the little newsletter box that slides in from the
corner: all of it is chrome, not content. The first job of any book
formatter is a ruthless one — decide what the *text* actually is.

The second job is gentler: keep the pictures, keep the emphasis, keep the
code samples and the blockquotes, and set them in a way that ink would
respect.
